\begin{abstract}
One paragraph. What the problem is, what you built, what you did with it, and
what you found --- in that order, because that is the order a reviewer reads
in. Say the finding rather than promising it: \say{selection, not authoring,
became the bottleneck} is a result, and \say{we discuss implications} is not.
Four to six sentences is usually the whole of it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Open with the thing that is true and awkward. A paragraph that could begin any
paper in the field is a paragraph a reviewer skips, so name the specific state
of affairs your work is a response to, and cite the work that established it
\cite{example2020}.

Then the turn: what nobody has done, and why the obvious way of doing it does
not work. \SystemName is what this paper calls the thing we built; naming it
once with a macro means renaming it later costs one line.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep]
  \item A design for \SystemName, and the three decisions in it that a reader
        could disagree with.
  \item An implementation, described in \autoref{sec:system}, that runs on
        ordinary hardware.
  \item A study with twelve participants (\autoref{sec:study}), and the
        finding that changed what we thought we were building.
\end{itemize}

One participant said it better than we have:

\begin{blockquote}
I stopped drawing things. I started looking for the thing I would have drawn,
and I found it about half the time --- and the other half I found something
better.
\end{blockquote}

\sayit{I stopped drawing things} is, we think, the whole result. \note{Decide
before submission whether this quotation belongs here or in the discussion.}

\section{\SystemName}
\label{sec:system}

Describe what it does before how it does it. A reader who cannot picture the
interface cannot follow the implementation.

\subsection{Design goals}

Three at most, each one a sentence, each one something you could have failed at.

\subsection{What it is made of}

\autoref{tab:conditions} is a table in the shape most papers need one: booktabs
rules, a spanning header, a shaded column, and one column that wraps.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{l M{1.6cm} L{2.9cm}}
\toprule
& \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{What we measured}} \\
\cmidrule(lr){2-3}
Condition & \sh Trials & Notes \\
\midrule
Baseline & \sh 120 & The interface as it was before any of this. \\
\SystemName & \sh 118 & The same task, the same people, a week later. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Two conditions, and what each of them cost.}
\label{tab:conditions}
\end{table}

Two figures that belong side by side go in one float, as subfigures:

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \begin{subfigure}{0.47\columnwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Source/Figures/before.png}
    \caption{Before.}
  \end{subfigure}
  \hfill
  \begin{subfigure}{0.47\columnwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Source/Figures/after.png}
    \caption{After.}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{The same task in both conditions.}
  \label{fig:conditions}
\end{figure}

A figure that needs the whole width of the page is a starred float. It waits
for the top of a page, which is where the class puts it:

\begin{figure*}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Source/Figures/walkthrough.png}
  \caption{A walkthrough, three panels wide, spanning both columns.}
  \label{fig:walkthrough}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Implementation}

Say what it is written in, what it depends on, and what it cannot do. If there
is a formula worth writing down, write it down:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:score}
  s(r) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \, f_i(r)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i},
\end{equation}
and then say in words what \autoref{eq:score} means, because most readers will
take your word for it.

\section{Study}
\label{sec:study}

\subsection{Participants}

Twelve people (P1--P12), recruited how, paid what, and screened for what. Say
the thing that makes them the right or the wrong people to ask.

\subsection{Procedure}

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item What they did first.
  \item What they did next.
  \item What you asked them afterwards.
\end{enumerate}

Each session lasted about ninety minutes.\footnote{Which is the longest a
person will do this without resenting it.}

\subsection{Findings}

Number the findings and give each one a sentence you could put in the abstract.

\subsubsection{Selection became the bottleneck}

Eleven of twelve participants stopped composing and started choosing
(\autoref{tab:conditions}). What they wanted was not a better editor but a
better ordering.

\subsubsection{The second finding}

State it, evidence it, and resist explaining it here --- that is what the
discussion is for.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What this means for tools}

Two or three implications, each one something a reader could act on. An
implication nobody could act on is a summary.

\subsection{Limitations}

Say the real ones. A reviewer who finds a limitation you did not mention stops
trusting the parts you did.

\section{Conclusion}

Two sentences. What you did, and what it tells us.

\section{Materials}
\label{app:materials}

Sections after \verb|\appendix| are lettered rather than numbered, which is the
only thing that command does that a reader can see. Put the interview script,
the coding scheme and the questionnaire here.

\subsection{Interview script}

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item Tell me about the last time you did this without our tool.
  \item What did you expect to happen?
  \item What happened?
\end{enumerate}
